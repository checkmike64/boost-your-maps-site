Who shows up on Google Maps for pest control in 15 US cities

On November 11, 2026, we searched “pest control” on Google Maps from downtown in 15 US cities. Listings with fewer than 10 Google reviews took 24 of the 45 top-three spots. Half of the top three were within 0.55 miles of where we searched. For spots 4 to 20, half were within 1.52 miles.

By Michael Moll, founder of Boost Your Maps. Published November 11, 2026. Last updated November 11, 2026. 294 listings, 15 cities, one search per city.

  • 169 of 294 listings had fewer than 10 reviews. In Atlanta and Miami, they held all three top spots.
  • 33 of 129 listings within a mile of the search point made the top three. Of the 165 farther out, 12 did.
  • About 1 in 3 top-three listings with a website linked to a page about the city. In spots 4 to 20 it was about 1 in 7.

Listings with fewer than 10 reviews held more than half the top spots

Most of the listings we saw from downtown had fewer than 10 reviews: 169 of 294, and 81 had none. They held 24 of the 45 top-three spots. Charlotte and Mesa were the only cities where every top-three company had 10 reviews or more. In Houston and Miami, none of the first 20 listings had 100 or more.

Review counts didn't line up neatly with the top three. 17 of the 74 listings with 100 or more reviews made it, against 24 of the 169 with fewer than 10, and 4 of the 51 in between. Few-review listings held the most top spots overall. There were more of them, and 103 of the 169 sat within a mile of the search point.

Of the 24 few-review listings in the top three, 21 also had no website or had the city's name in the business name. We use those signs together as a rule for a likely lead-generation listing, one that may exist to collect calls and pass them to other companies. 97 of the 294 listings fit it. We didn't call them or check who owns them, and a new local company can fit the rule too.

Listings near the search point made the top three far more often

Google says local results depend on how well a listing matches the search, how far it is from the searcher, and how well known the business is. Our downtown search point stood in for the searcher. 33 of the 129 listings within a mile of it made the top three. Of the 165 listings farther out, 12 did.

We also lined up each city's listings by distance and by place in the results. In all 15 cities the two orders leaned the same way, closer listings higher, though only weakly in Dallas and Miami. Review counts leaned that way in 10 cities and the other way in 5.

None of the 45 top-three listings hid its address, while 22 of the other 249 did. For those 22, we measured to the map pin Google placed for them.

Source for the three factors: Google Business Profile Help, “Tips to improve your local ranking on Google.”

Few top-three listings linked to a page about the city

A Google profile can carry a website button. 18 of the 45 top-three listings had none. Of the 27 that did, 18 sent people to a homepage and 8 to a page about the city, about 1 in 3. In spots 4 to 20, 27 of the 179 listings with a website linked to a city page, about 1 in 7.

That's a small group of 8, and companies with city pages may place well for other reasons. One search can't show that the link itself helps.

Which pest people searched most changed from city to city

Keyword Planner shows what people in each city typed from November 2025 to October 2026. Mosquito searches came out ahead in 7 cities, though in Knoxville only by 160 to 150, inside Keyword Planner's rounding. Termite led in Houston, New Orleans and Phoenix, scorpion in Las Vegas and Mesa, bed bug in Chicago and Philadelphia, and rodent in Los Angeles.

In 9 cities Keyword Planner counts rodent searches together with “pest control”, so rodent may lead in some of them without showing. Ant and roach searches are in the data file and left off the chart, because they were small in most cities. Searches for “pest control” plus the city name hit their slowest month between November and February in 14 of the 15 cities. In New Orleans, September tied with three winter months.

What customers tagged as the price

Google lets reviewers tag what they paid, using set ranges. We read the 20 newest reviews of 23 established companies, and 134 of the 460 carried a price tag. 91 of the 134 price tags were under $200, and 54 price tags were under $100. Another 43 price tags fell in Google's top range, $200 or more. That range has no ceiling, so it collects every bigger job.

The tags don't say whether a price was for one visit, a first service or a yearly plan. 6 of the 11 termite price tags said $200 or more. More reviewers rated the price than gave a dollar range. Of those 194, 175 called it reasonable or great, 13 weren't sure and 6 called it overpriced.

What this means if you run a pest control company

The top three leaned toward listings close to the search point, and many of those had few reviews. Your customers search from all over your service area, and we didn't search from there. So check where your company shows up from the towns you serve.

What this study does not show

We ran one search from one point in each city on November 11. A search from the suburbs would likely show other companies, and results change from day to day. We picked 15 cities where lots of people search for pest control. Most are in the South and the West, plus Chicago and Philadelphia, and other cities may look different. In several cities, Keyword Planner's October 2026 counts jump well above the months before. We'll check that month again when Google updates it. We counted the free Maps list only, so the two paid ads in Mesa and any Local Services Ads are left out. The price tags show what customers chose to tag, and we never saw a bill.

The files leave out business names, websites and addresses because the study is about kinds of listings. We kept the full rows on file, so we can check any row for an editor who asks.

How we did it

  1. We searched “pest control” once per city on November 11, 2026, from city hall, or from downtown in Miami and Charlotte. The exact points are in the cities file.
  2. We used OpenSEO's local results tool, which reads Google Maps through DataForSEO. It asks for mobile results in English at map zoom 13, with no signed-in user.
  3. We kept the first 20 results in each city and looked hardest at the first three, the ones a searcher sees first. Mesa showed two paid ads above its 20 results, which we left out. Knoxville showed 14 results. That gave 294 listings.
  4. For each listing we recorded its place, reviews, star rating, categories, photos and whether it showed an address. We also measured the straight-line distance from the search point to its map pin, and noted where its website button goes.
  5. We flagged a listing as likely lead generation when it had fewer than 10 reviews plus no website or the city name in the business name. The same rule ran in every city, and we didn't check it any other way.
  6. Search counts come from Google Ads Keyword Planner for each city. Yearly figures add up the 12 months from November 2025 to October 2026.
  7. In each city we read the 20 newest Google reviews of the two highest-placed pest control companies with 100 or more reviews. Atlanta, Phoenix and San Antonio gave one each and Houston and Miami none. The price ranges and pest types are Google's own review tags.

The method note lists every field in the files and how we filled it. It also covers the review sample city by city.
