Dental SEO: 8 fixes to get your practice found on Google

Dental SEO is how a dental practice gets found by nearby people who search Google for a dentist. It covers the map and the results under it. The practice's profile, website, reviews and links from other sites decide how often it shows up. Distance to the searcher counts too.

The 8 fixes for a dental profile, in order

1. What categories fit a dental practice?

Pick the main category that fits most of your visits. For a general practice, that's “Dentist.” Google offers extras such as “Cosmetic dentist,” “Emergency dental service,” “Pediatric dentist,” “Periodontist,” “Orthodontist,” “Endodontist,” “Oral surgeon,” “Dental implants provider” and “Teeth whitening service.” Add an extra only for work your office does.

2. How do you list treatments?

Name each treatment the way a patient says it: dental implants, teeth whitening, root canal, dentures, children's checkups, emergency visits. Write a short plain description for each, and start with what your city looks up most. Keyword Planner counted 390 monthly searches for dental implants in Fort Worth and 320 for pediatric dentistry, with periodontist at 140.

3. What should your profile name and address say?

Google's rules say the profile name must be the one on your front sign and your paperwork. Adding “Best Dentist” or a city name breaks that rule, and a profile can be suspended for it. Show the office address, because patients walk in there. A virtual office or a mailbox that makes you look closer to another neighborhood can get the profile suspended too.

4. What should the website button open?

Send it to a page about that one office. Put your phone number, hours and new patient steps near the top. End the link with a tag such as ?utm_source=gbp so your site stats can count the visits it sends. An office with its own profile needs its own page. Add a page for each main treatment, like implants, emergency visits and kids. Each page should load quickly on a phone and carry its own photos and answers, since Google's spam rules go after pages that only swap in a new city name.

5. How should you ask for reviews?

After a visit, ask each patient to leave one, using a short link you can text or print. Google bans picking only happy patients and bans paid reviews. Reply to every review within a few days. Don't say the writer is your patient or mention any treatment, because patient privacy rules cover your replies.

6. What photos and posts help?

Each month, add photos of your front desk, treatment rooms, equipment and team, once each person says yes. A patient's photo needs written permission first. Posts should cover what you offer right now, such as new patient exams, emergency slots and kids' appointments.

7. Why must your details match across the web?

Directories and map sites copy your name, address and phone. Start with Apple Maps, Bing, Yelp, Healthgrades, your state or provincial dental society's find-a-dentist page, and the provider lists of insurance plans you accept. Correct old phone numbers, old suite numbers and doctors who left.

8. Where do links come from?

The best links come from your dental society and the local chamber of commerce. Schools and teams you sponsor, dental suppliers that profile their customers, and local news can link to you too. Google asks that paid links, including sponsorships, carry a sponsored mark. Buying links from sellers breaks Google's link spam rules.

Sources for steps 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8: Google, “Guidelines for representing your business on Google,” Google Search's spam policies and the Google Maps user-contributed content policy. Step 1 uses Google's 2025 category list.

When should a dental practice start, and what should it track?

Plan on 60 to 90 days for profile work to show on the map. Houston's dentist searches were highest from November to January.

The Performance tab in your profile counts calls, website clicks and direction requests by month. Your site stats, using the tag from step 4, show what those visitors do next. Keep your own office line as the main phone number, since Google says you must control it. Each month, compare those three counts with how many people searched for a dentist in your city.
